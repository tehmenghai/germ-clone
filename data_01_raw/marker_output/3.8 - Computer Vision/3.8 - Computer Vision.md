## 1x1 Kernel
Eﬀectively just performs a linear combination of the
input features at each spatial location.
It doesn't capture spatial relationships or local patterns
because it only looks at a single pixel at a time.

## 2x2 Kernel
Captures a small, localized region of the input, but it's
still too small to eﬀectively capture complex patterns or
high-level features.
It also introduces a lot of parameter redundancy and
ineﬃciency for capturing larger spatial patterns.

## 4x4 Kernel
Captures a larger region of the image compared to
1x1 or 2x2 kernels.
However, 2x2 and 4x4 are generally not preferred
because odd-sized ﬁlters symmetrically divide the
previous layer pixels around the output pixel.
If this symmetry is not present, there will be
distortions across the layers which happens when
using an even sized kernels

## Padding
1.

## Preserving Spatial Dimensions: Padding ensures
that the spatial dimensions (height and width) of
the input feature maps are preserved after
applying convolutions. Without padding, the
spatial dimensions would shrink after each
convolutional operation. This can lead to a loss of
information, especially at the edges of the input.
2.

## Edge Information: Padding helps preserve
information at the borders or edges of the input
image. When the convolutional kernel is applied
near the boundaries, some parts of the kernel
might not have access to data outside the image,
so padding ﬁlls those gaps.
https://medium.com/@minhazc.engg/padding-and-strides-in-cnn-58dc56493887
3.

## Control over Output Size: Padding allows
control over the size of the output feature maps.
Without padding, the output size decreases with
each convolution, but by adding padding, you
can ensure the output size is either the same as
the input or controlled to a speciﬁc dimension.

## Pooling Layers
Pooling layers play a crucial role in convolutional neural networks
by reducing the spatial size of the representation and the
number of parameters, which helps control overﬁtting. They
work by summarizing the features present in regions of the
feature map generated by convolutional layers.

## Max Pooling
Max pooling is the most common pooling operation, and it
involves sliding a window (usually 2x2 or 3x3 in size) over the
feature map and taking the maximum value within the window
as the output.

## Average Pooling
Average pooling takes the average of the elements within the
window. It is less common than max pooling

## Normalization is a pre-processing step done before training a machine

## learning model. Its goal is to make sure that all input features are on a

## similar scale. For example, one feature might represent height in

## centimeters (like 170), while another is a ratio (like 0.8).

## •

## Cost function will be more symmetrical (links back to gradient descent)

## Batch Normalisation

## Batch Normalization is a technique used inside a neural network, typically

## between layers, to normalize the outputs (activations) of those layers

## during training.

## Instead of normalizing the entire dataset, Batch Norm works on

## mini-batches (small subsets of the data) during training.

## Batch Norm standardizes the activations (outputs) of each layer:

## 1.

## For each mini-batch, it calculates the mean and standard deviation of

## the activations.

## 2.

## It then normalizes the activations so that they have zero mean and

## unit variance.

## When to apply BatchNorm?
1. Convolutional Layers — Very common
●
Placed after a convolution and before the activation:
Conv2D → BatchNorm → ReLU
●
Helps stabilize training, reduce internal covariate shift, and allow for higher learning rates.
●
Widely used in architectures like ResNet, VGG, etc.
2. Fully Connected / Linear Layers — Less common, but valid
●
Used in deep networks with multiple dense layers (e.g., MLPs or transformer heads).
●
Format:
Linear → BatchNorm1d → ReLU
●
Especially helpful when the input features vary signiﬁcantly in scale.

## Transfer Learning

## Transfer learning is a machine learning technique where a model developed for one task is

## reused as the starting point for a model on a second task. It is a popular approach in deep

## learning where pre-trained models are used as the starting point on computer vision (and

## natural language processing) tasks given the vast compute and time resources required to

## develop neural network models on these problems and from the huge jumps in skill that

## they provide on related problems.

## How does Transfer learning work?

## The ﬁrst layers of a CNN learn to recognize general

## features such as vertical edges, the subsequent

## layers, the horizontal edges, and then maybe these

## features are combined to recognize the corners,

## circles, etc.

## These high-level features are independent of the

## type of entity we need to recognize. Computer

## vision models don't just “learn” exactly what, for

## Instead, models break down images into small

## When Transfer Learning may not be ideal
Transfer learning is a powerful technique in deep learning, where you use a pre-trained model on a large
dataset and ﬁne-tune it for your speciﬁc task. However, there are situations where transfer learning
might not be ideal.
Here are some scenarios where transfer learning might not be the best option:

## 1. Your Task and Dataset Are Very Diﬀerent from the Pre-trained Model’s Task:
●

## Problem: Transfer learning works best when the pre-trained model was trained on a dataset that
is similar to your dataset in terms of domain, content, and characteristics. If the pre-trained model
was trained on a completely diﬀerent type of data (e.g., a model trained on natural images like
ImageNet being used on medical images), transfer learning may not perform well.
●

## Example: Using a model trained on ImageNet for classifying medical scans (like X-rays or MRI
images) might not yield good results because the feature representations learned from natural
images may not be applicable to medical images.


<!-- page 51 -->
Copyright © 2024 Nanyang Technological University. All rights reserved.

## When Transfer Learning may not be ideal

## 2. Insuﬃcient Data for Fine-tuning:
●

## Problem: Transfer learning usually requires some amount of ﬁne-tuning with your speciﬁc
data. If you have very little data for your task, ﬁne-tuning might lead to overﬁtting, as the
pre-trained model's weights might not generalize well to the small dataset.
●

## Example: If you're trying to ﬁne-tune a model with a few hundred images, the model may not
adapt properly and could perform worse than training from scratch.

## 3. When the Task Requires Domain-Speciﬁc Knowledge:
●

## Problem: If your task requires very specialized domain knowledge that the pre-trained model
hasn’t encountered, transfer learning might not work well. Models trained on general datasets
(like ImageNet) might not capture domain-speciﬁc features that are crucial for your task.
●

## Example: Using a general pre-trained model for natural language processing tasks on a speciﬁc
legal document corpus might not work well because the pre-trained model hasn't been
exposed to the unique vocabulary and structures of legal texts.
