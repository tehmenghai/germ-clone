**Core concept:** Germayne framed the bias-variance tradeoff as a central theme in machine learning: when a model has high variance, its bias tends to go down, and when a model has high bias, its variance tends to go down.

**Bias (underfitting):**
- He described bias in a "hand-wavy" way as essentially underfitting.
- Example: linear regression fit to clearly nonlinear data — by definition it must draw a straight line, so no matter how much data you give it, it can't capture the true curved relationship.
- High bias means the model has too many assumptions/constraints, making it too rigid to capture the underlying pattern in the data. This is underfitting.

**Variance (overfitting):**
- He then contrasted this with a model that fits every single point perfectly, calling this the definition of overfitting.
- High variance means the model is too flexible and ends up capturing noise, not just signal.
- Key test: if you resample the data and retrain, an overfit model gives very different outputs each time — that instability is why it's called "high variance."
- He compared this to a student who memorizes exam answers: perfect on identical questions, but fails when the question changes slightly — "so good at your training data, but you learn nothing."

**The tradeoff itself:**
- You don't want a model that only works on training data (overfitting) nor one that gives generic outputs and learns nothing (underfitting) — you want to balance the two.
- As model complexity increases, bias naturally decreases (fewer assumptions, more flexibility) but variance increases as a tradeoff. He illustrated this with an error-vs-model-complexity graph.
- He noted this is a very common data science interview question ("what is bias-variance tradeoff?").

**Bullseye analogy:**
- Low bias, low variance → arrows consistently hit the bullseye (ideal).
- Low bias, high variance → arrows scattered around the bullseye on average, but wildly spread (inaccurate/unstable).
- High bias, low variance → arrows tightly clustered but consistently off-target (consistently wrong).
- High bias, high variance → the worst case, essentially no reliable model.

**Practical takeaway:** He cited Andrew Ng's famous point that one way to reduce variance is simply to use more (diverse) training data, since exposing the model to more relevant examples helps prevent overfitting — this also set up the later discussion of regularization as another way to address high variance.
